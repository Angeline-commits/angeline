How to read: Factor Loadings

  1. 1What the table shows. Each row is a survey item and each column a factor. A loading is the strength of the link between an item and a factor, like a correlation (from −1 to 1; the closer to ±1, the stronger). Blank cells are loadings below jamovi's display cutoff (0.30 by default), so each item here belongs clearly to one factor only (a "simple structure").
  2. 2Factor 1 collects satisfaction with courtesy of the staff (0.849), speed of check-in (0.825) and the handling of requests (0.795). All three are about dealing with staff and service, and all loadings are well above 0.70. A fitting label is Service (naming is the researcher's interpretation, not output).
  3. 3Factor 2 collects cleanliness of the room (0.952), hotel amenities (0.691) and comfort of the bed (0.640). All three are about the physical room and facilities, with loadings from fair to very high. A fitting label is Room and facilities.
  4. 4Uniqueness is the share of that item's variance (the spread of guests' responses to it) that the two factors do not account for (1 − communality). Courtesy at 0.295 means the factors account for 70.5% of its variance; cleanliness at 0.079 means 92%, so it is captured almost perfectly; comfort of the bed at 0.600 means only 40%, the weakest item. Low uniqueness is good; values above about 0.60 are often flagged.
  5. 5Method (footnote). Minimum residual extraction with an oblimin rotation. Oblimin is an oblique rotation: it lets the factors correlate, which is realistic for aspects of satisfaction. The loadings shown are pattern loadings. The correlation between the two factors is given in the Inter-Factor Correlations table below.

How to read: Factor Statistics (Summary)

  1. 1What "variance" means here. Guests do not all give the same ratings; their responses differ, and variance (the spread of guests' responses) is simply a measure of how much they differ. A factor "explains variance" when it accounts for ratings that move together: guests who rate the courtesy of staff highly also tend to rate check-in speed and request handling highly, and the Service factor captures that shared pattern.
  2. 2SS Loadings = 2.05 and 1.81 are the sums of the squared loadings on each factor: how much of the total variance (the spread of guests' responses across all six items) each factor accounts for, counted in "items". With six items, Factor 1 accounts for the equivalent of 2.05 items and Factor 2 for 1.81. Values above 1 mean a factor carries more than a single item's worth of information.
  3. 3% of Variance (jamovi's column name) = SS Loadings ÷ number of items: 2.05 ÷ 6 = 34.1% and 1.81 ÷ 6 = 30.1%. Each is the share of the total variance that the factor accounts for, so the two factors carry similar weight.
  4. 4Cumulative % = 64.2%. Together the two factors account for about 64% of the total variance (the spread of guests' responses) across the six items; a common benchmark in social science is 60%. The other 35.8% is specific to each item (the uniqueness values sum to 2.15, and 2.15 ÷ 6 = 35.8%).

How to read: Inter-Factor Correlations

  1. 1What it shows. Because the rotation was oblique (oblimin), the factors are allowed to correlate, and this table reports how strongly. The dashes are each factor compared with itself; with two factors there is a single number.
  2. 2r = 0.155 is a weak positive correlation. Squared, it is 0.155² ≈ 0.024, so the two factors overlap by only about 2.4%. Guests who rate the service a little higher also tend to rate the room a little higher, but service and room/facilities are largely separate dimensions of satisfaction.
  3. 3Why it matters. If factors correlate strongly (above roughly .70) they may really be one dimension; if they correlate moderately (about .30 or more) an oblique rotation is clearly justified. At .155 the factors are distinct, and an orthogonal rotation would be expected to give a very similar picture. Keeping the oblimin result is safe either way.

How to read: Bartlett's Test of Sphericity (check before interpreting the loadings)

  1. 1What it tests. The null hypothesis is that the items are not correlated at all: no question is related to any other. If that were true there would be no common factors to find.
  2. 2χ² = 117, df = 15. df = items × (items − 1) ÷ 2 = 6 × 5 ÷ 2 = 15, the number of distinct item pairs.
  3. 3p < .001 is below α = .05, so we reject the null: the items are correlated enough for factor analysis. (With large samples this test is almost always significant, so read it together with the KMO.)

How to read: KMO Measure of Sampling Adequacy

  1. 1Overall MSA = 0.693. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure (0 to 1) compares the correlations among items with their partial correlations; higher means the way items move together is more likely due to common factors. A common guide: above .90 marvelous, .80 meritorious, .70 middling, .60 mediocre, below .50 unacceptable. At .693 the sampling is middling, comfortably above the .50 minimum.
  2. 2Item MSAs = 0.610 to 0.747. Each item has its own value, and all are above .50, so no item needs to be dropped. The lowest is cleanliness of the room (0.610), still acceptable.
  3. 3Verdict: Bartlett's test and the KMO both support running a factor analysis on these items.

How to read: Scree Plot with parallel analysis

  1. 1What the plot shows. The vertical axis is the eigenvalue (how much of the variance (the spread of guests' responses) each possible factor captures), the horizontal axis the factor number. The solid line is your data; the dotted line is "Simulations": the eigenvalues you would get from random data of the same size (parallel analysis).
  2. 2How many factors to keep. Keep the factors whose data point lies above the simulated line. Factor 1 (about 2.1 against 1.2) and Factor 2 (about 1.1 against 0.4) are above it; Factor 3 (about 0.0 against 0.2) is below. The data therefore support two factors, the number used in the table above.
  3. 3The elbow. The data line falls steeply from factor 1 to factor 3 and then flattens, which also points to two factors.
  4. 4Values below zero (factors 4 to 6) are normal for minimum residual extraction and simply mean those factors explain nothing.
